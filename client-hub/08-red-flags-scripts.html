Red Flags & Handling Scripts

Identify warning signs early and respond professionally with proven scripts

Critical Red Flags

Stop immediately - these indicate high risk of project failure or payment issues

"We'll have more budget after launch"

Critical
Why This Is a Red Flag

This almost always means they don't have budget now and are hoping the project will generate enough revenue to pay you. You become an unpaid investor in their venture with zero equity.

Handling Script

"I appreciate you sharing your vision! To ensure we can deliver the quality you deserve, we work on a payment-for-work model rather than revenue-sharing arrangements. This protects both of us and ensures we're fully focused on building the best possible product. What budget do you have confirmed for the initial phase?"

Alternative Actions
Offer reduced MVP scope Suggest phased approach Decline politely

Unclear ownership or multiple decision makers

Critical
Why This Is a Red Flag

Projects with unclear decision-making authority lead to endless revision cycles, conflicting feedback, and delayed approvals. You'll spend more time managing stakeholder politics than building.

Handling Script

"To ensure smooth collaboration, could you help me understand your decision-making process? Who will be the single point of contact for approvals and feedback? In our experience, projects move fastest when we have one person who can make final decisions on design and functionality."

Alternative Actions
Request RACI matrix Add stakeholder meeting clause Build in revision limits

"We need this yesterday" with no flexibility

Critical
Why This Is a Red Flag

Artificial urgency often indicates poor planning, unrealistic expectations, or a client who will blame you when their arbitrary deadline isn't met. Rush jobs also have higher defect rates and lower satisfaction.

Handling Script

"I want to help you hit your deadline! To do that well, I need to understand: what's driving this specific date? Is it a product launch, investor meeting, or market opportunity? Understanding the 'why' helps us identify what's truly essential for that date versus what could come in a fast-follow release."

Alternative Actions
Propose MVP for deadline Add rush fee (50-100%) Define non-negotiable scope cut

Asking for detailed spec before signing

Critical
Why This Is a Red Flag

They may be collecting free consulting to either do it themselves, share with a cheaper competitor, or use your thinking without compensation. Detailed specs are work product that has value.

Handling Script

"I'd love to provide a detailed specification! That level of detail is actually part of our Discovery & Strategy phase, which is a separate engagement. This typically takes 1-2 weeks and results in a comprehensive technical specification, architecture plan, and accurate project estimate. Would you like me to send over the Discovery proposal?"

Alternative Actions
Offer paid discovery phase Provide high-level scope only Share case study instead

Demanding NDA before sharing the idea

Critical
Why This Is a Red Flag

This usually indicates either paranoia, inexperience with how tech projects work, or an idea that's not actually defensible. Ideas are worthless; execution is everything. They may also have unrealistic expectations about IP.

Handling Script

"I understand wanting to protect your idea! In my experience working with hundreds of clients, I've never shared or stolen an idea — my reputation depends on trust. For initial conversations, I'm happy to keep things confidential without a formal NDA. If we move forward, we'll include confidentiality clauses in our standard agreement. Would you be comfortable sharing a high-level overview?"

Alternative Actions
Use your standard NDA Ask them to share high-level only Decline if they insist on complex NDA

Bad-mouthing previous agencies/developers

Critical
Why This Is a Red Flag

If every previous partner was "incompetent" or "didn't understand," the common denominator is the client. They'll likely say the same about you eventually. This often indicates unclear requirements, scope creep tolerance, or unrealistic expectations.

Handling Script

"I'm sorry you had a frustrating experience. To make sure we don't repeat those issues, could you help me understand: what specifically went wrong? Was it communication, quality, timeline, or something else? And what would 'success' look like differently with us?"

Alternative Actions
Document concerns in contract Build in extra review cycles Add change request premium

Warning Signs

Proceed with caution - address these before signing

"While you're at it, can you also..."

Warning
Why This Is a Warning

Scope creep tendencies in discovery predict scope creep in the project. If they're adding requests before you've even started, expect constant additions throughout. This needs to be addressed with clear boundaries and change order processes.

Handling Script

"Great idea! I'm noting that for Phase 2. For this initial engagement, let's focus on [original scope] to ensure we deliver something valuable within your timeline and budget. Once that's solid, we can absolutely discuss adding those features. Does that work for you?"

Mitigation Strategies
Fixed-scope contract Defined change order process Buffer in estimate

"I need to run this by my partner/board/team"

Warning
Why This Is a Warning

Not necessarily bad, but indicates you're not talking to the decision maker. Decisions will take longer, and their partner/board may have different priorities. You may need to re-sell the project to someone you haven't met.

Handling Script

"Of course! To help you make the case, would it be helpful if I joined that conversation? I can answer any technical or strategic questions directly. Alternatively, I can prepare a one-pager summary that addresses the key points decision-makers typically care about. What would be most useful?"

Mitigation Strategies
Request decision-maker meeting Prepare executive summary Set decision timeline

"Another agency quoted half your price"

Warning
Why This Is a Warning

Price-focused clients often undervalue quality and become difficult when issues arise. They may also be bluffing to negotiate, or comparing apples to oranges (offshore vs. onshore, junior vs. senior team).

Handling Script

"I appreciate you sharing that! Price differences usually come down to three things: scope, quality, or team seniority. Could you share what's included in their quote? I want to make sure we're comparing the same thing. If budget is a primary concern, I can suggest ways to reduce scope while still achieving your core goals."

Mitigation Strategies
Stand firm on value Offer reduced scope option Walk away if needed

"You're the expert, just make it great"

Warning
Why This Is a Warning

Flattering, but dangerous. "Great" is subjective. Without clear success criteria, you'll never know when you're done, and they'll keep requesting changes because it's not matching their undefined vision.

Handling Script

"I appreciate the trust! To deliver 'great,' I need to understand what that means for your specific situation. Let's define 3-5 measurable success criteria. For example: 'The site loads in under 2 seconds,' 'Users complete checkout in under 3 clicks,' or 'We launch by March 1st.' What metrics matter most to you?"

Mitigation Strategies
Define success metrics Get design approval in writing Limit revision rounds

Taking weeks to respond during sales

Warning
Why This Is a Warning

Slow communication during the sales phase predicts slow communication during the project. This leads to delayed approvals, blocked progress, and frustration. If they can't prioritize now, they won't prioritize later.

Handling Script

"I want to make sure we're set up for success! What's a realistic response time I can expect during the project? For smooth progress, we typically need feedback within 48-72 hours on deliverables. Does that align with your team's availability?"

Mitigation Strategies
Add response SLA to contract Build delays into timeline Auto-approval clause

"It HAS to be built in [specific tech]"

Warning
Why This Is a Warning

Technology requirements without business justification suggest either a technically-minded client who will micromanage, someone influenced by a friend's opinion, or an existing developer who's lobbying for their preferred stack.

Handling Script

"I'm familiar with [tech]! Just so I understand the full picture: what's driving that requirement? Is it team expertise, existing infrastructure, or a specific capability? Sometimes there are alternatives that might better serve your needs, but I want to understand the constraints before suggesting anything."

Mitigation Strategies
Document tech decisions Explain trade-offs clearly Get sign-off on limitations

Green Flags

Positive indicators that suggest a great fit

Clear problem definition and success metrics

Why This Is Positive

Clients who can articulate their problem clearly and define what success looks like have done their homework. They'll be easier to work with because everyone's aligned on the goal.

How to Reinforce

Acknowledge their clarity and document their success criteria in the proposal. Reference these metrics throughout the project to maintain alignment.

Budget approved and realistic for scope

Why This Is Positive

Pre-approved budgets mean faster decision-making and less back-and-forth with finance teams. Realistic budgets indicate they value quality and understand market rates.

How to Reinforce

Thank them for their transparency and provide a detailed breakdown showing value for their investment. Consider offering a small bonus or additional value add.

Single decision-maker with authority

Why This Is Positive

Clear decision-making authority means faster approvals, consistent feedback, and no "design by committee" syndrome. Projects move smoothly when one person owns the outcome.

How to Reinforce

Confirm their authority early and direct all major decisions through them. Create clear escalation paths for edge cases.

Previous agency experience (good or learning)

Why This Is Positive

Clients who've worked with agencies before understand the process, know how to provide feedback, and have realistic expectations. Even bad experiences mean they've learned what they don't want.

How to Reinforce

Ask what worked well and what they'd want different. Use their past experience to customize your process and address their specific concerns proactively.

Quick, thoughtful responses

Why This Is Positive

Fast communicators during sales will be fast communicators during the project. This indicates they're prioritizing the project and have bandwidth to engage properly.

How to Reinforce

Match their responsiveness. Thank them for their engagement and set expectations that you'll maintain this pace throughout the project.

Referred by a past client

Why This Is Positive

Referrals come pre-sold on your value and have realistic expectations set by someone who's worked with you. They're also more likely to trust your process and recommendations.

How to Reinforce

Thank the referrer (and offer a referral bonus if you have one). Ask the new client what specifically prompted them to reach out based on the referral.

Has examples/references of what they like

Why This Is Positive

Clients who bring references have thought about their preferences and can articulate direction. This accelerates design exploration and reduces subjective back-and-forth.

How to Reinforce

Dig into why they like specific examples. Identify patterns across their references to understand their underlying preferences and aesthetic sensibilities.

Long-term vision with growth potential

Why This Is Positive

Clients with a roadmap beyond this project are thinking strategically. This often leads to ongoing work, retainer relationships, and the opportunity to see your work grow over time.

How to Reinforce

Ask about their 6-12 month vision. Build the initial project with extensibility in mind and document decisions that support future phases.

Common Scenarios & Playbooks

Step-by-step guides for handling tricky situations

The Price Negotiator

They love your work but keep pushing back on price, asking for discounts or trying to reduce scope to hit an arbitrary budget.

1 Acknowledge their budget constraint without apologizing for your price
2 Ask what's driving the budget number — is it a hard limit or a preference?
3 Offer scope reduction options rather than price reduction
4 If they still push, stand firm: "This is our best price for this scope"
5 Be willing to walk away — discount clients rarely become good clients

The Scope Creeper

Every meeting includes "one more thing" and they keep adding features while expecting the same price and timeline.

1 Acknowledge the new idea enthusiastically: "That's a great feature!"
2 Immediately frame it as additive: "Let me add that to the Phase 2 list"
3 If they push for Phase 1, explain the trade-off clearly
4 Pull out the change order process: cost + timeline impact
5 Document everything — email confirmation of any scope changes

The Ghoster

They were super excited, said they wanted to move forward, then disappeared. No response to emails or calls.

1 Send a brief check-in after 3-5 business days
2 Follow up once more after another week with a clear CTA
3 Send a "closing the loop" email after 2 weeks of silence
4 Move them to nurture list — they may return later
5 Don't take it personally — priorities shift, budgets change

The Competitor Bluffer

"We have three other proposals and need to decide by Friday" — using competitive pressure to rush your decision or get a discount.

1 Acknowledge without panic: "I understand you're evaluating options"
2 Don't match arbitrary deadlines — quality proposals take time
3 Ask what criteria they're using to decide (hint: it's rarely just price)
4 Focus on your unique value, not competing on their terms
5 If they choose someone else, wish them well and stay in touch

The Spec Fisher

Asking for detailed technical specifications, wireframes, or strategy documents before committing to any paid engagement.

1 Explain that detailed specs are valuable work product
2 Offer a paid Discovery phase (smaller commitment, clear deliverable)
3 Provide high-level scope and estimate without detailed specs
4 Share case studies and past work as proof of capability
5 If they insist on free specs, politely decline

The Emergency Project

"Our developer quit / our launch is in 2 weeks / we just got funding and need to move NOW" — genuine urgency with real constraints.

1 Assess if the urgency is real or manufactured
2 If real, determine what's actually needed by the deadline
3 Apply rush pricing (typically 25-100% premium)
4 Get faster approval cycles and decision authority in writing
5 Define a realistic MVP scope — cut ruthlessly

Graceful Decline Templates

Professional ways to say "no" while maintaining relationships

Budget Mismatch

When to Use

Their budget is significantly below your minimum or what the project requires.

Template

Hi [Name], Thank you for considering Craefto Lab for your project. After reviewing the scope and your budget, I don't think we're the right fit for this engagement — our approach and team structure work best with projects in the $[X]+ range. That said, I don't want to leave you without options: • [Freelancer/agency name] does great work at a more accessible price point • [Platform] has vetted freelancers for projects like yours • Consider a phased approach starting with [critical feature] I'd love to stay connected — please reach out when your budget aligns with a larger engagement. Best, [Your name]

Scope Mismatch

When to Use

The project doesn't align with your core services or expertise.

Template

Hi [Name], Thanks for reaching out about your [project type]. While it sounds like an interesting challenge, it's outside our core expertise in [your specialty]. You'd be better served by a team that specializes in [their need]. A few recommendations: • [Agency/freelancer 1] — they're great at [specialty] • [Agency/freelancer 2] — strong portfolio in [related area] If you have future projects involving [your specialty], I'd love to chat. Best, [Your name]

Timeline/Capacity

When to Use

You're at capacity or their timeline doesn't work with your availability.

Template

Hi [Name], Thank you for thinking of Craefto Lab! Unfortunately, our current project load means we couldn't give your project the attention it deserves within your timeline. Our next availability opens up in [timeframe]. If your timeline is flexible, I'd love to reconnect then. In the meantime, here are some trusted colleagues who might have availability: • [Referral 1] • [Referral 2] Let me know if that later timeline could work, or if you'd like an introduction to either of those teams. Best, [Your name]

Red Flags / Bad Fit

When to Use

You've identified red flags that make this a risky engagement (without saying that directly).

Template

Hi [Name], After our conversations, I've been reflecting on whether Craefto Lab is the best fit for your project. I want to be honest: I don't think we're the right partner for this one. To deliver our best work, we need [specific thing missing — clear requirements, single decision maker, etc.]. Without that alignment, I'm concerned we wouldn't meet your expectations. I'd recommend looking for a partner who [has different strengths/structure]. [Optional referral] I wish you the best with the project. If circumstances change, don't hesitate to reach out. Best, [Your name]

Already Committed Elsewhere

When to Use

You're already in talks with a competitor or the project conflicts with existing client relationships.

Template

Hi [Name], Thank you for reaching out about your project. I need to be transparent: we have an existing relationship that would create a conflict if we were to work together. I take these commitments seriously, which is why I'm being upfront rather than proceeding and potentially compromising either engagement. I'm happy to recommend some excellent alternatives: • [Referral 1] • [Referral 2] If your situation changes or you have projects in other areas, I'd welcome the opportunity to work together. Best, [Your name]

Simple "Not Right Now"

When to Use

A general decline when you don't need to give specific reasons.

Template

Hi [Name], Thank you for considering Craefto Lab! After reviewing your project, I don't think we're the right fit at this time. I appreciate you reaching out and wish you the best finding the perfect partner for this work. If things change on our end, I'll reach out. Best, [Your name]

Quick Decision Framework

Always Decline If

  • No budget / "we'll pay after launch"
  • Requests free detailed specifications
  • Multiple failed agency relationships
  • Refuses to define success criteria
  • Demands you work for equity only
  • Red flags significantly outweigh green

Proceed with Caution If

  • Budget is tight but confirmed
  • Timeline is aggressive but achievable
  • Multiple stakeholders (get SPOC assigned)
  • Vague requirements (scope with buffer)
  • Slow communication (add response SLA)
  • First time working with agencies

Fast-Track If

  • Referral from past successful client
  • Clear requirements and success metrics
  • Approved budget matching scope
  • Single decision-maker identified
  • Quick, thoughtful communication
  • Long-term vision and growth potential

Key Phrases to Use

  • "To ensure success, I need to understand..."
  • "Let me add that to the Phase 2 list"
  • "What's driving that specific requirement?"
  • "In my experience, projects succeed when..."
  • "I don't think we're the right fit, but..."
  • "Here's what that would require..."
